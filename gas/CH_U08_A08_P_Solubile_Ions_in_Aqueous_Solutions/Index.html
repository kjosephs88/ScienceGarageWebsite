Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions

As you already know, when you put table salt into water and stir, the salt will dissolve. This happens because of the interaction between the polar water molecules and the ionic charges within the salt. Table salt is made of positive sodium ions ($ \ce{Na+} $) and negative chloride ions ($ \ce{Cl-} $). The negative oxygen end of a water molecule attracts the positive sodium ions, and the positive hydrogen end attracts the negative chloride ions. Millions of water molecules work together to pull individual ions away from the ionic sample.

However, not all salts (a term synonymous with ionic compounds) will dissolve in water. Whether a salt will dissolve depends on the ion combination in the compound. The Reference Table Selected Ions Forming Aqueous Solutions can help us determine if an ionic compound will be soluble.

Let's take a look at what the reference table tells us about sodium chloride ($ \ce{NaCl} $). We must first realize that the compound is made of $ \ce{Na+} $ and $ \ce{Cl-} $ ions. Looking at our table, we notice right away that $ \ce{Na+} $ is a Group 1 ion. The table shows that Group 1 metals always form soluble aqueous solutions. Make sure to consult the periodic table when identifying Group 1 ions so you don't miss those not explicitly listed, such as potassium, $ \ce{K+} $. Because sodium is on this list, sodium chloride is soluble!

Let's look at another example: $ \ce{(NH4)3PO4} $. Upon inspection, we can see that ammonium phosphate is made of an ammonium ion, $ \ce{NH4+} $, and a phosphate ion, $ \ce{PO4^3-} $. Looking over the chart, we find that the $ \ce{NH4+} $ ion is listed as an ion that forms aqueous solutions. This means the compound is soluble in water.

What about an insoluble compound? If a compound does not contain any of the ions listed on this table, it is likely an exception that forms a precipitate (a solid). For example, lead(II) chloride, $ \ce{PbCl2} $. Neither the lead ion ($ \ce{Pb^{2+}} $) nor the chloride ion ($ \ce{Cl-} $) are on the list of selected ions. Because it lacks any of our 'always soluble' ions, we can identify $ \ce{PbCl2} $ as the insoluble compound when compared to others that contain Group 1 metals or nitrates.
