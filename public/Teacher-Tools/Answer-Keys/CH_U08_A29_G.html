Phenomenon: Electrolytes in Sports Drinks

During intense physical activity, the human body loses water and vital minerals through sweat. Sports drinks are heavily marketed for their ability to rapidly replenish these lost "electrolytes." A typical sports drink contains a mixture of water, glucose sugar (\(\ce{C6H12O6}\)), sodium chloride (\(\ce{NaCl}\)), and potassium citrate (\(\ce{K3C6H5O7}\)).

1. Which component of the sports drink is classified as a nonelectrolyte when dissolved in water? (1 point)
  • (1) Sodium chloride, \(\ce{NaCl}\)
  • (2) Potassium citrate, \(\ce{K3C6H5O7}\)
  • (3) Glucose, \(\ce{C6H12O6}\)
  • (4) Water, \(\ce{H2O}\)
Correct Answer: (3) Glucose, \(\ce{C6H12O6}\)
2. When \(\ce{NaCl}\) dissolves in water, the positive sodium ions (\(\ce{Na+}\)) are attracted to which part of the water molecules? (1 point)
  • (1) The partially positive hydrogen atoms
  • (2) The partially negative oxygen atoms
  • (3) The partially negative hydrogen atoms
  • (4) The partially positive oxygen atoms
Correct Answer: (2) The partially negative oxygen atoms
3. Adding dissolved salts to water changes its colligative properties. Compared to pure water, how do the boiling and freezing points of the sports drink compare? (1 point)
  • (1) Higher boiling point and lower freezing point
  • (2) Lower boiling point and higher freezing point
  • (3) Higher boiling point and higher freezing point
  • (4) Lower boiling point and lower freezing point
Correct Answer: (1) Higher boiling point and lower freezing point
4. Using a mathematical or computational model, calculate the molarity of a 500. mL (0.500 L) sports drink that contains 0.025 moles of dissolved \(\ce{NaCl}\). Show your numerical setup and the calculated result. (1 point)
Result: 0.050 M
Phenomenon: Behavior of Carbonated Soft Drinks

A bottling plant manufactures carbonated soft drinks by dissolving carbon dioxide gas, \(\ce{CO2(g)}\), into water at a cold temperature of 6.0°C under a sealed high pressure of 3.5 atm. When a consumer buys the bottle and opens it at room temperature (22°C), the pressure above the liquid decreases sharply to 1.0 atm, causing rapid effervescence (bubbling) as the gas escapes the solution.

5. Based on the carbonated soft drink phenomenon, what occurs to the solubility of the carbon dioxide gas when the consumer opens the bottle cap and drops the system pressure from 3.5 atm to 1.0 atm? (1 point)
  • (1) The solubility of the gas increases.
  • (2) The solubility of the gas decreases.
  • (3) The solubility of the gas remains completely unchanged.
  • (4) The solubility of the gas decreases, then solid solutes precipitate.
Correct Answer: (2) The solubility of the gas decreases.
6. If the opened soda bottle is left sitting out on a warm counter, it goes flat much faster than if it were kept cold inside a refrigerator. Which statement correctly models the effect of temperature on gas solubility? (1 point)
  • (1) Solubility of gas solutes increases as temperature increases.
  • (2) Solubility of gas solutes decreases as temperature increases.
  • (3) Solubility of solid solutes decreases as temperature increases.
  • (4) Temperature affects pressure but has no impact on particle solubility paths.
Correct Answer: (2) Solubility of gas solutes decreases as temperature increases.
Phenomenon: Mitigating Hazardous Ice on Winter Roads

During sub-zero winter weather, municipal road management crews distribute chemical salts onto icy highways to lower the freezing point of water and melt accumulated ice. A lab technician prepares two test solutions to analyze this colligative property: Solution A contains 1.0 mole of molecular glucose, \(\ce{C6H12O6}\), dissolved in 1.0 liter of water. Solution B contains 1.0 mole of ionic magnesium chloride, \(\ce{MgCl2}\), dissolved in 1.0 liter of water.

7. When solid magnesium chloride, \(\ce{MgCl2}\), dissolves completely in the water to form Solution B, into how many total moles of individual dissolved solute particles does 1.0 mole of the formula unit split? (1 point)
  • (1) 1.0 mole of particles
  • (2) 2.0 moles of particles
  • (3) 3.0 moles of particles
  • (4) 4.0 moles of particles
Correct Answer: (3) 3.0 moles of particles
8. Compared to Solution A (molecular \(\ce{C6H12O6}\)), Solution B (ionic \(\ce{MgCl2}\)) will cause which structural change to the boiling point and melting point profiles of the water? (1 point)
  • (1) Solution B has a lower boiling point and a higher melting point.
  • (2) Solution B has a higher boiling point and a lower melting point.
  • (3) Solution B has an identical boiling point and melting point profile.
  • (4) Solution B has a lower boiling point and a lower melting point.
Correct Answer: (2) Solution B has a higher boiling point and a lower melting point.
9. Explain, in terms of atomic structure, chemical bonds, and dissolved particles, why an ionic salt compound like magnesium chloride, \(\ce{MgCl2}\), alters the colligative physical properties of water significantly more than an equal number of moles of a molecular compound like glucose. (2 points)
Answer: Magnesium chloride is an ionic compound that completely dissociates into 3 moles of separate ions (\(\ce{Mg^{2+}}\) and two \(\ce{Cl-}\)) per mole of formula unit in water. Glucose is a molecular compound with covalent bonds that remains as 1 mole of intact molecules when dissolved. Because colligative properties depend on the total number of solute particles rather than their identity, \(\ce{MgCl2}\) produces a greater particle concentration, altering water's physical properties more significantly.
Phenomenon: Laboratory Experimentation

Students in a foundational chemistry laboratory analyze various liquid mixtures and unknown analytical standards. They systematically measure electrical conductivity, test behaviors against solid reactive metal strips, and modify sample sets using formal mathematical concentration variables.

10. A student dissolves exactly 0.40 moles of solid sodium nitrate solute into a volume of water to create an aqueous electrolyte solution with a targeted final concentration of 2.0 M. Using mathematical or computational model, calculate the total volume, in liters, of the resulting solution. Show your numerical setup and the calculated result. (1 point)
Result: 0.20 L
11. A student reacts a strip of active magnesium metal, \(\ce{Mg(s)}\), with an aqueous solution of hydrochloric acid, \(\ce{HCl(aq)}\). Which gas product is yielded from this single replacement reaction? (1 point)
  • (1) \(\ce{O2(g)}\)
  • (2) \(\ce{H2(g)}\)
  • (3) \(\ce{CO2(g)}\)
  • (4) \(\ce{Cl2(g)}\)
Correct Answer: (2) \(\ce{H2(g)}\)
Phenomenon: Acid Rain and Statues

Acid rain containing dilute sulfuric acid, \(\ce{H2SO4(aq)}\), falls on historical marble statues, which are primarily composed of solid calcium carbonate, \(\ce{CaCO3(s)}\). Over decades, this chemical interaction causes the solid marble to dissolve and deteriorate, threatening cultural heritage sites worldwide.

12. According to the Arrhenius theory, an aqueous solution of sulfuric acid contains which of the following as the only positive ion in solution? (1 point)
  • (1) hydrogen ion
  • (2) hydroxide ion
  • (3) sulfate ion
  • (4) calcium ion
Correct Answer: (1) hydrogen ion
13. As the acidic rainwater reacts with and is neutralized by the solid basic calcium carbonate rock, what happens to the pH of the water running off the statue? (1 point)
  • (1) It decreases below 7
  • (2) It increases towards 7
  • (3) It remains strictly at 1
  • (4) It increases above 7
Correct Answer: (2) It increases towards 7
14. In the chemical reaction between the acid rain and the calcium carbonate, one of the products is calcium sulfate. Calcium sulfate is classified chemically as: (1 point)
  • (1) an acid
  • (2) a base
  • (3) a salt
  • (4) a non-electrolyte
Correct Answer: (3) a salt
15. Sulfuric acid dissolves in water to form hydronium and hydrogen sulfate ions according to the following reversible equation:
\(\ce{H2SO4(aq) + H2O(\ell) <=> H3O+(aq) + HSO4^-(aq)}\)

Based on the Bronsted-Lowry theory, identify the two acids in this reaction. (1 point)
Answer: \(\ce{H2SO4(aq)}\) and \(\ce{H3O+(aq)}\)
Phenomenon: Determining the Acidity of Vinegar

A student performs a titration to determine the concentration of acetic acid, \(\ce{HC2H3O2(aq)}\), of a sample of store-bought white vinegar. They add a few drops of bromothymol blue indicator to the vinegar flask and titrate it with a standard 0.10 M solution of \(\ce{NaOH}\). The student's experimental data is recorded in the table below:

Volume of Vinegar Sample 10.0 mL
Initial \(\ce{NaOH}\) Burette Reading 2.5 mL
Final \(\ce{NaOH}\) Burette Reading 27.5 mL
Molarity of \(\ce{NaOH}\) Standard 0.10 M
16. What color will the bromothymol blue indicator turn when the titration reaches the endpoint? (1 point)
  • (1) Colorless
  • (2) Yellow
  • (3) Green
  • (4) Blue
Correct Answer: (3) Green
17. Which of the following defines a base according to the Bronsted-Lowry theory? (1 point)
  • (1) An electron pair donor
  • (2) An electron pair acceptor
  • (3) A proton (\(\ce{H+}\)) donor
  • (4) A proton (\(\ce{H+}\)) acceptor
Correct Answer: (4) A proton (\(\ce{H+}\)) acceptor
18. In the space below, write the net ionic equation for the neutralization reaction between the sodium hydroxide and acid in the vinegar. (1 point)
Correct Answer: \(\ce{H+ + OH- -> H2O}\)
19. Based on the data table provided, determine the volume of \(\ce{NaOH}\) used, and then calculate the molarity of the acetic acid in the vinegar. Show your work with equations and substitution with units. (2 points)
Result: 0.25 M
Work:
\(\text{Volume of NaOH} = 27.5\text{ mL} - 2.5\text{ mL} = 25.0\text{ mL} = 0.0250\text{ L}\)
\(n_{\text{NaOH}} = MV = (0.10\text{ M})(0.0250\text{ L}) = 0.0025\text{ mol}\)
\(M_{\text{acid}} = \frac{n}{V} = \frac{0.0025\text{ mol}}{0.0100\text{ L}} = 0.25\text{ M}\)
20. Identify the chemical formula of the salt produced in the neutralization reaction between \(\ce{HC2H3O2(aq)}\) and \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\). (1 point)
Correct Answer: \(\ce{NaC2H3O2}\)
